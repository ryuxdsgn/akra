## Ideas

## [ ] 001 — Where "OK" came from
- hook: "OK" started as a joke spelling in old newspapers.
- angle: a 1830s Boston newspaper fad for misspelled abbreviations; "oll korrect" became OK
- sources: Online Etymology Dictionary entry on "OK"; Merriam-Webster word history of "OK"
- visual: medium

## [ ] 002 — Why it's called a sandwich
- hook: The sandwich is named after a place, via an earl.
- angle: named after the 4th Earl of Sandwich; one story says he ate it at the card table, so it is told as a story, not fact; the earl is drawn as a generic cartoon in period costume
- sources: Online Etymology Dictionary entry on "sandwich"; Britannica entry on the sandwich
- visual: high

## [ ] 003 — Where the weekday names come from
- hook: Your week is named after planets and old gods.
- angle: Sunday, Monday and Saturday come from the Sun, Moon and Saturn; Tuesday to Friday from Germanic gods; told neutrally as names only
- sources: Online Etymology Dictionary entries on each weekday; Britannica entry on the week
- visual: high

## [ ] 004 — How the fork reached the dinner table
- hook: For centuries, Europeans thought forks at dinner were strange.
- angle: table forks spread slowly from the Byzantine world through Italy to the rest of Europe
- sources: Britannica entry on the fork; a museum collection page on historical cutlery
- visual: high

## [ ] 005 — Were Roman soldiers paid in salt?
- hook: "Salary" comes from salt. The payday story is shakier.
- angle: the word comes from Latin for salt; one story says soldiers were paid in salt, but historians say there is no clear evidence
- sources: Online Etymology Dictionary entry on "salary"; a university classics department explainer on Roman pay
- visual: high

## [ ] 006 — Why clocks run clockwise
- hook: Clocks turn this way because of shadows.
- angle: on a Northern Hemisphere sundial the shadow moves this way, and mechanical clocks copied it
- sources: a national observatory or time museum explainer on sundials; Britannica entry on the sundial
- visual: high

## [ ] 007 — Where denim and jeans got their names
- hook: Your jeans are named after two cities.
- angle: "denim" from a fabric "de Nîmes" in France; "jeans" from a cotton cloth linked to Genoa
- sources: Online Etymology Dictionary entries on "denim" and "jeans"; a museum collection page on denim
- visual: high

## [ ] 008 — Where the word "robot" came from
- hook: "Robot" was born in a 1920 play.
- angle: from a Czech play about artificial workers; the word comes from Czech "robota", forced labor; the writer is drawn as a generic character with a name label
- sources: Online Etymology Dictionary entry on "robot"; Britannica entry on the play R.U.R.
- visual: high

## [ ] 009 — Why pencil "lead" isn't lead
- hook: There has never been lead in your pencil.
- angle: pencils use graphite; early users thought the shiny mineral was a kind of lead, and the name stuck
- sources: Britannica entry on the pencil; a science or design museum page on pencil history
- visual: high

## [ ] 010 — Why September isn't the seventh month
- hook: September means "seven", but it's the ninth month.
- angle: the early Roman calendar started in March, so September to December were months seven to ten
- sources: Online Etymology Dictionary entry on "September"; Britannica entry on the Roman calendar
- visual: high
